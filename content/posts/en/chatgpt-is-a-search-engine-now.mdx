## The Short Version

ChatGPT is not just a chatbot anymore.

Europe just made that explicit.

On August 31, the European Commission [designated ChatGPT](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-designates-chatgpt-reddit-roblox-under-digital-services-act) as a Very Large Online Search Engine under the Digital Services Act. Reddit and Roblox were designated as Very Large Online Platforms in the same move.

That sounds like regulatory filing cabinet music.

It is actually a product diagnosis.

The Commission says the services declared at least 45 million average monthly users in the EU, crossing the threshold for the DSA's highest tier. OpenAI's own [DSA help page](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8959649-eu-digital-services-act-dsa) says ChatGPT search had about 159.1 million average monthly active recipients in the EU for the six-month period ending March 31, 2026.

The useful story is not:

"Europe regulates another American tech company."

The useful story is:

An AI assistant that answers questions, searches the web, recommends options, shows ads, shapes purchases, and influences decisions is no longer just a model in a chat box.

It is a distribution surface.

And distribution surfaces get platform rules.

Very normal. The text box has become a search engine with feelings.

## The label matters less than the job

There will be a tempting argument about the label.

Is ChatGPT really a search engine?

Is it a platform?

Is it a chatbot?

Is it an operating layer?

Yes. Annoyingly.

The product does not fit cleanly into old internet categories because the category is changing under it. A normal search engine takes a query, retrieves links, ranks them, and sends users elsewhere. ChatGPT can retrieve information, summarize it, synthesize it, remember context, compare options, recommend a next step, and increasingly act on the user's behalf.

That makes it less like a list of links and more like a decision interface.

If I ask for the best CRM for a small team, a travel itinerary, a coding library, a meal plan, a tutor, a therapist directory, a financial explanation, or a product comparison, I am not only asking for information.

I am letting the assistant structure the choice.

That is why the DSA designation matters.

Regulators are not only looking at the model.

They are looking at the service.

The service has users. It has ads. It has search. It has ranking. It has moderation. It has reporting flows. It has minors. It has recommender-like behavior, even when the interface looks like one answer instead of a feed.

The model may be the engine.

The product is the road.

## The DSA is asking platform questions

The Commission's [VLOP and VLOSE guidance](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/dsa-vlops) lists the boring things that become very interesting at scale.

Designated services have four months to comply with additional obligations. They need to assess and mitigate systemic risks connected to illegal content, fundamental rights, public security, elections, minors, public health, and mental and physical wellbeing. They need independent audits, compliance functions, transparency around ads and recommender systems, data sharing with authorities, and access for vetted researchers studying systemic risks.

That sounds like paperwork until you put it next to the product.

What is an ad in a conversation?

What is a recommender system when the answer is generated on the fly?

What counts as illegal content when the model paraphrases, summarizes, or combines sources?

What is a ranking system when the user never sees the ranked list?

How do researchers audit a product where every answer depends on context, memory, tools, model version, location, personalization, safety policy, and sometimes browsing?

Very relaxing questions. Perfect for a Tuesday meeting with Legal.

But these are exactly the questions serious AI products now have to answer.

The DSA is not only asking whether ChatGPT said a bad thing.

It is asking whether ChatGPT, as a system, can understand and reduce the risks created by its own scale.

That is a different bar.

## Ads make the classification feel obvious

The timing is funny.

On August 18, OpenAI said [ChatGPT Ads would expand across Europe](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-ads-expands-across-europe/). OpenAI said the expansion covers 31 European markets, that ads appear only for Free and Go users, and that Plus, Pro, and Enterprise remain ad-free. It also said ads do not influence ChatGPT's answers, advertisers do not see conversations, and ads are labeled and separate from answers.

Those are the right promises.

They are also the promises of a company that knows it now operates a decision surface.

OpenAI's ads post says people come to ChatGPT while planning trips, choosing software, furnishing homes, learning concepts, or starting hobbies. It says marketers can reach people while they explore, evaluate, and make decisions.

Exactly.

That is the regulatory point.

A search engine with ads is not just infrastructure.

A social feed with ads is not just hosting.

And an AI assistant with ads is not just a model answering questions.

It is an attention and decision market.

OpenAI can say, sincerely, that advertising does not influence the generated answer. But the deeper question is not only whether the ad changes the model output. It is whether the product as a whole changes what users consider, trust, click, buy, ignore, or believe.

That is why old platform rules keep finding their way into new AI products.

The interface changed.

The power did not disappear.

## Builders should stop pretending the category is neutral

This matters beyond OpenAI.

If you are building an AI product, your regulatory category is not determined by what you call it in the pitch deck.

"Copilot" does not make it harmless.

"Assistant" does not make it private.

"Agent" does not make it enterprise software.

"Search" does not make it objective.

"Recommendation" does not make it a minor feature.

The category comes from the job the product does in the user's life.

Does it retrieve information from the web?

Does it rank options?

Does it recommend products, people, content, software, travel, health resources, financial actions, jobs, or schools?

Does it show ads?

Does it shape decisions for minors?

Does it host user-generated content, bots, tools, stores, apps, or public outputs?

Does it act for the user?

Does it combine search, memory, personalization, and action?

Then you may have built more than a chatbot.

Congratulations. You have product-market fit and a governance problem.

## The AI Act is not the whole story

One mistake is to treat this as "AI regulation" in the narrow sense.

The EU already has the AI Act. That matters for model providers, general-purpose AI, high-risk use cases, documentation, evaluation, and deployment duties.

But the ChatGPT DSA designation shows that the AI Act is not the only regulatory map.

AI products also inherit platform law, consumer law, privacy law, competition law, advertising law, child-safety rules, sector rules, and eventually procurement rules.

That is not because regulators are confused.

It is because AI systems are swallowing old product categories.

A chatbot becomes search.

Search becomes recommendation.

Recommendation becomes advertising.

Advertising becomes consumer protection.

Memory becomes privacy.

Plugins become platform access.

Agents become software operators.

The clean "model layer" story was useful for investors and architecture diagrams.

It is less useful once the product is deciding what the user sees and does.

## What teams should ask

If your company builds or buys AI products, ask less:

What model powers it?

Ask:

- What role does this product play in the user's decision?
- Does it retrieve, rank, recommend, or advertise?
- Are ads separated from answers in a way users actually understand?
- Can the system explain why a source, product, vendor, or option appeared?
- Does the service have appeal and reporting flows for illegal or harmful outputs?
- Can researchers or auditors study systemic risks without seeing private user data?
- What happens when the same prompt produces different answers because of memory, location, model version, tools, or personalization?
- Are minors using the product, and does the system know?
- Does the product need a public ad repository, transparency reports, or recommender disclosures?
- If an enterprise embeds the assistant, who owns the compliance surface: the model provider, the application vendor, or the customer?

This is not as satisfying as a benchmark chart.

It is also much closer to the real work.

## The bottom line

The EU did not make ChatGPT important today.

Users already did that.

OpenAI did not become a search company because Brussels used the VLOSE label. It became search-like because people use ChatGPT to explore the web, compare options, summarize sources, ask follow-up questions, and make decisions. The Commission is now attaching old platform obligations to the new interface because the interface has reached old-platform scale.

That is the lesson.

The next generation of AI products will not be governed only by model cards, safety evals, and API terms.

They will be governed by the jobs they take over.

If the assistant behaves like search, it gets search questions.

If it behaves like a recommender, it gets recommender questions.

If it behaves like an ad platform, it gets ad-platform questions.

If it behaves like an agent, it gets operational-risk questions.

The chat box was a wonderful disguise while it lasted.

But the disguise is coming off.

ChatGPT is a search engine now.

More importantly, it is a decision layer.

And decision layers do not get to stay weightless forever.
