## Update: two checked proofs are not yet a consensus

OpenAI and Anthropic now say their models have independently refuted the Exponential Time Hypothesis.

Both have published Lean artifacts.

That is not the same as the computer-science community accepting the result.

The distinction matters because ETH is not an obscure benchmark. It is a foundational assumption used to argue that hundreds of problems cannot be solved substantially faster. Refuting it would change the map of complexity theory.

It would not prove P equals NP. It would not make SAT easy. And it would not suddenly make today's hard optimization problems practical.

Anthropic's [own research artifact](https://github.com/anthropics/formal-math/tree/main/eth) is explicit about that last point: the claimed algorithm is purely asymptotic and says nothing about solving formulas in practice.

This is exactly the kind of result where “Lean checked it” sounds like the end of the story.

It is actually the beginning of a more interesting review process.

## The formal proof has a boundary

A proof assistant answers a precise question:

Does this formal derivation establish this formal statement inside this logic?

That is an extraordinarily valuable question. It removes a huge class of arithmetic mistakes, missing cases, and persuasive hand-waving. A model cannot bluff Lean's kernel.

But the kernel does not know what the paper meant to claim.

Anthropic says this directly. Its short [`MainResults.lean`](https://github.com/anthropics/formal-math/blob/main/eth/MainResults.lean) file states the theorems a reader should inspect. Lean checks the proofs behind those statements, but, as the repository notes, no machine checks that the file says what the paper says.

The project makes that gap unusually inspectable. It pins Lean and Mathlib, records the standard axioms used, maps paper statements to formal counterparts, and runs the proof through `nanoda`, a second independently written kernel.

That gives us strong evidence about the derivation.

It does not automatically establish the paper-to-theorem correspondence, the relevance of every encoding choice, or the interpretation of the result.

OpenAI exposes a different boundary. Its [Lean scope note](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/lean/docs/378.md) verifies an exact counting algorithm for fixed-width CNF formulas in subexponential time. But the checked bound includes an extra `log log n` factor compared with the sharper bound in the accompanying manuscript.

Both bounds would be enough to refute ETH if the formal statement, proof, and interpretation all survive scrutiny.

They are still not identical claims.

This is what formal verification looks like when the headline is larger than the theorem file: the proof may be checked while the scientific claim still needs careful human reconciliation.

## OpenAI is publishing a review process, not a victory lap

OpenAI's most important decision may not be the Lean release.

It is the language around it.

The company [describes its new manuscripts](https://openai.com/index/building-scientific-understanding-with-academic-communities/) as raw research data that must be converted into scientific understanding. For the ETH claim, it has formed an external group led by Russell Impagliazzo, Ramamohan Paturi, and Ryan Williams.

Impagliazzo and Paturi helped formulate ETH in the first place.

The participants are unpaid, have no nondisclosure agreements, and are free to publish their own conclusions. OpenAI has agreed to release the raw data and fund a conference where the field can examine the claim and its consequences.

That does not certify the result.

It does something more useful at this stage: it separates discovery from acceptance.

The model produced an artifact. The proof assistant checked a formal derivation. Domain experts now have to understand the argument, compare the formal and informal claims, place it in the literature, and decide what follows.

Those are different jobs.

Collapsing them into “AI solved ETH” would throw away the most mature part of the release.

## “Verified” now needs levels

AI labs are producing technical artifacts faster than expert communities can absorb them. A binary label will not survive that volume.

We need a validation ladder:

1. **Kernel-checked:** the formal proof passes a small trusted checker.
2. **Statement-audited:** experts agree the formal theorem expresses the intended claim.
3. **Correspondence-audited:** the paper, theorem, encodings, and implementation describe the same result.
4. **Independently understood:** outside researchers can reconstruct the argument rather than merely rerun the checker.
5. **Scientifically situated:** the community understands what the result changes, what it leaves open, and whether earlier work depended on the discarded assumption.

The OpenAI and Anthropic releases appear strong at the first level.

They provide unusually good material for the next ones.

They have not completed the ladder simply by publishing green checks.

This is not skepticism about formal methods. It is the reason formal methods are useful. They turn one large, fuzzy question into several smaller questions whose answers can be examined separately.

## The new publication unit is the review stack

The old scientific paper bundled discovery, explanation, and persuasion into one document.

AI-generated research needs a thicker publication unit:

- the natural-language argument,
- the exact formal statement,
- the machine-checked proof,
- pinned dependencies and allowed axioms,
- a map between the paper and the formalization,
- independent expert review,
- and a public record of what was accepted, corrected, or rejected.

The proof artifact is not a decorative appendix.

The review process is not a ceremonial delay.

Together, they are the product.

If the ETH claims hold, this will be a major result in complexity theory and a striking demonstration of AI-assisted discovery.

If a hidden mismatch changes or defeats them, the releases will still teach us something important about how to evaluate machine-generated science.

Either way, the lesson is already visible.

When models can generate proofs faster than people can understand them, validation cannot end at “the file compiles.”

That is where understanding begins.

## Update: the proof checker changed the bottleneck

Anthropic and Theorem just produced around 180 million lines of machine-checked proof.

Nobody has to read them.

That is not a joke about documentation. It is the point.

On October 22, Anthropic [published a formal-verification project](https://www.anthropic.com/research/formal-verification) in which Claude patched bugs and then proved no crashes were reachable for 239 fuzzing entry points across 123 open-source libraries, under a defined set of conditions. The proofs operate on compiled RISC-V instructions rather than source code. Rocq, a proof assistant, checks that every proof satisfies its theorem.

Anthropic says the successful proofs contain roughly 180 million lines of Rocq. A typical one is around 600,000 lines long.

In an ordinary review process, that volume would be absurd.

Here, volume is mostly a compute bill. The proof checker does not get tired, skim the repetitive section, or become impressed by confident notation. Either the proof satisfies the theorem inside Rocq's logic or it does not.

This is a real change in the validation economics described in this article.

It is not the end of validation.

It moves the scarce human judgment to a smaller and more important surface:

What, exactly, did we ask the machine to prove?

## The result is stronger than another security scan

Fuzzing looks for an input that makes a program fail. It can run trillions of tests and still never establish that no failing input exists.

Anthropic and Theorem turned existing OSS-Fuzz targets into formal statements about compiled binaries. For the successful targets, Claude generated proofs covering every modeled input within the stated limits, not merely the inputs a fuzzer happened to try.

The process also surfaced bugs that testing had missed. A proof cannot close while a reachable counterexample remains, so failed attempts become a way to locate the code or assumption preventing the theorem from being true.

That combination matters.

An AI scanner says, "I found another possible bug."

A checked proof says, "For this exact binary, this exact entry point, this exact property, and these exact assumptions, there is no counterexample."

The second statement is narrower.

It is also much stronger.

The [technical appendix](https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/files/4zrzovbb/website/6867eadf760258fa8b42a504600cd3c036d16358.pdf) says Claude generated about one billion lines of Rocq while attempting the work. Roughly 180 million lines ended up in successful proofs. More than half of a typical proof is machine-emitted data, and checking one proof can take from 30 minutes to several hours of CPU time.

This does not look like a human proof engineer becoming slightly faster.

It looks like proof production becoming an industrial workload.

## The theorem statement is now the product boundary

The public headline is easy to compress into "Claude proved 123 libraries cannot crash."

That is too broad.

The actual claims concern particular fuzzing entry points in particular static RISC-V binaries running under a formal Linux-process model. The work assumes single-threaded execution, a fixed memory budget, musl rather than the dynamically linked glibc environment common in deployed Linux systems, and modeled operating-system behavior.

It does not prove that each entire library is correct.

It does not cover code unreachable from the selected entry point.

It does not catch every class of memory or logic error that AddressSanitizer and the harness fail to express.

It does not prove that callers use the library correctly.

And the [public proof repository](https://github.com/theorem-labs/oss-verify-proofs) is currently a subset of the reported work: 60 entry points across 25 libraries, not all 239 entry points across 123 libraries. That is enough to make outside inspection possible, but not enough to independently reproduce the full headline.

The repository is admirably precise about its own boundary. It says the released proofs concern specific static RISC-V binaries, that its Linux Process Semantics model is incomplete, and that none of the published proofs rules out a run that never terminates.

This is what responsible formal verification sounds like.

Not "the software is safe."

"This theorem holds for this artifact inside this model."

## You can check a proof and still prove the wrong thing

Formal verification changes which errors remain possible.

It largely removes the need to trust the author of the proof. Rocq checks the proof mechanically. A model cannot bluff the kernel with elegant prose.

But the checker cannot tell us whether the theorem captures the property users care about.

It cannot tell us whether the formal RISC-V and Linux semantics match the real machine closely enough.

It cannot tell us whether a no-crash property excludes the security failure in the threat model.

It cannot tell us whether the deployed x86 or ARM build behaves like the verified RISC-V binary.

It cannot tell us whether a patch preserved the behavior maintainers wanted.

Anthropic flags the last risk directly. Claude was allowed to patch code before proving it, which creates an obvious reward-hacking route: make the theorem easy by deleting or bypassing the behavior. The team says it penalized patches, rewarded minimal behavioral changes, logged them, and audited them.

Those are useful controls.

They are also evidence that the specification and change policy are now part of the security perimeter.

Once proof generation becomes cheap, the valuable human work is defining the theorem, reviewing the trusted model, examining patches, and deciding whether the verified artifact corresponds to production.

The proof can be perfectly valid while the assurance claim is wrong.

## The public artifact matters more than the line count

One hundred eighty million lines is the irresistible number.

The repository is the more important release.

Theorem publishes theorem statements, binaries, provenance records, pinned tool versions, allowed axioms, checking instructions, and continuous verification workflows. A third party can inspect the claim, trace the binary to its build inputs, and run the checker without trusting Claude.

That is the right architecture for AI-generated technical work.

Do not ask a reviewer to read everything the model produced.

Reduce the trusted surface. Pin it. Publish it. Make the decisive claim executable. Let a small checker reject the enormous artifact.

The remaining gap should be visible, not smoothed over by a summary.

In this case, the visible gaps include the difference between the full reported result and the public subset, between RISC-V and common production architectures, between fuzz-target coverage and library correctness, and between crash freedom and broader security.

Those caveats do not cancel the achievement.

They define it.

## Formal verification may become a normal build stage

Theorem is now [offering OSS Verify](https://theorem.dev/oss-verify/) free to qualifying open-source projects. Maintainers can submit their build recipe and fuzz targets, receive private bug reports discovered along the way, and eventually receive Rocq proofs about their binaries.

That points toward a practical workflow:

1. Write a test that fails when a property is violated.
2. Fuzz it to find cheap counterexamples quickly.
3. Use agents to patch or explain the remaining failures.
4. Convert the harness into a theorem about the compiled artifact.
5. Let a proof checker establish that no covered counterexample remains.
6. Preserve the theorem, binary provenance, assumptions, and checker version beside the release.

Testing does not disappear.

It becomes the first, inexpensive filter and a source of specifications.

Formal verification does not certify the whole product.

It turns selected security claims into build artifacts that can fail deterministically.

That is much more useful than treating "formally verified" as a badge.

## The new bottom line

Claude did not prove that 123 open-source libraries are bug-free.

Anthropic and Theorem demonstrated something more credible and, in practice, more consequential: frontier models can generate enormous proofs about real compiled software, and a small independent kernel can check those proofs without trusting the model.

The bottleneck has moved.

We no longer need a human to read every proof step.

We need humans to choose the right property, inspect the trusted semantics, review the patches, connect the verified binary to production, and state the result without inflating it.

The future of technical validation may involve far more machine-generated evidence than any person could consume.

That can work.

But only if the trusted claim becomes smaller as the evidence pile becomes larger.

## Update: the validator needs validation

Google has now turned the bottleneck described in this article into a product.

On October 14, Google Research [launched Deep Review publicly](https://research.google/blog/analyzing-technical-documents-with-deep-review/). Google says an earlier version was used across more than 16,000 opt-in submissions to STOC, ICML, NeurIPS, and ICLR.

The service accepts PDFs and plain text, gives each user two complimentary reviews per week, and offers an API for higher-volume workflows. Google says it can inspect mathematical reasoning, logical consistency, experimental design, citations, and comparisons with prior work. It is also positioning the system beyond academic papers, for design documents, presentations, legal agreements, and financial reports.

This is the sensible response to AI-generated technical abundance.

If models make claims faster, use models to help check claims faster.

But there is a dangerous product translation hiding inside that sentence.

"The model reviewed it" can very easily become "the document was verified."

Those are not the same state.

Deep Review looks useful. The evidence behind it is meaningful. The architecture is more serious than asking the same chatbot to criticize its own answer.

It is still an AI-generated review that a qualified person has to evaluate.

The validation bottleneck has not disappeared.

It has gained an API.

## This is a review pipeline, not one clever prompt

Deep Review uses a four-stage agentic system.

A segmenter divides the document into logical sections. A budgeting stage assigns more compute to dense material. Specialized agents inspect the sections in parallel while retaining access to the full document. A final agent synthesizes the findings, checks consistency across sections, and uses external grounding tools.

That design matters.

A long technical document is not one question. A proof may depend on a definition 30 pages earlier. An experiment may contradict a claim in the introduction. A chart may not support the conclusion written beside it. A single model call can run out of useful reasoning budget, focus on the easy sections, or accept an impressive-looking statement without testing it.

Google's system distributes the attention and then recombines the results.

This is the same lesson we keep learning about agents: the model matters, but the harness determines whether the model can do sustained work.

The interesting product is not Gemini saying, "I found three concerns."

It is the machinery that decides where to look, how much effort to spend, what evidence to retrieve, and how to merge conflicting findings.

That makes Deep Review more credible than a review prompt.

It does not make the output true by construction.

Several coordinated agents can still share the same blind spot. A synthesis agent can clean up a false critique until it sounds more authoritative. Grounding can confirm that a cited paper exists without proving that the paper supports the claim. More inference can improve recall while creating more plausible false positives for a human to investigate.

Two AI agents checking each other are not peer review.

They are a meeting.

## The results are promising and narrower than the product

Google's [Paper Assistant Tool research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28277) gives us more than a launch demo.

On a filtered subset of the SPOT benchmark containing 29 known equation or proof errors across 26 retracted mathematics and computer-science papers, the pipeline detected 89.7% of the errors. A zero-shot Gemini 3.1 Pro review detected 55.2%.

That is a substantial result.

It is also a Google-authored evaluation of a Google system on a small, selected subset, using a custom model-based grader whose decisions were audited by one of the paper's authors. It measures recall on known errors. It does not establish how many false allegations the system would produce across ordinary correct papers, unfamiliar fields, adversarial documents, or legal and financial work.

The conference pilots provide useful real-world evidence.

In the [ICML retrospective](https://blog.icml.cc/2026/03/30/retrospective-on-pat-x-icml-2026-ai-paper-assistant-program/), 92.1% of survey respondents said they would use the tool again. Among respondents with theoretical results, 35.4% said it found a significant gap that took more than an hour to fix. Among those with experiments, 31% said the feedback prompted new experiments.

Those numbers are impressive.

Another number deserves equal billing.

In the paper's combined STOC and ICML survey, only 55.8% and 64.8% of respondents, respectively, described the feedback as mostly or entirely grounded.

That does not make the system bad.

It tells us what job the system is ready for.

Deep Review is a powerful preflight review. It can surface places where an author should slow down, reproduce a result, explain an assumption, rerun an experiment, or ask an expert.

It is not a certification stamp.

## The pilot boundary was doing important work

The conference deployments were carefully bounded.

Authors opted in. The feedback was private. It arrived before submission. It was kept separate from the official review process. Authors remained responsible for deciding which findings were correct. The [NeurIPS program](https://blog.neurips.cc/2026/04/21/neurips-supports-authors-with-googles-paper-assistant-tool-pat/) explicitly warned that the model could flag correct statements as wrong or miss real flaws.

That is a good operating model.

The AI helps the author find work.

It does not decide whether the paper is accepted.

The public launch stretches the system into broader territory. Google now suggests reviewing legal and financial documents, where an apparently rigorous critique can influence contracts, compliance, investments, and disputes.

The public evidence does not yet establish equivalent performance in those domains.

Checking that two clauses conflict is useful.

Determining which clause is enforceable is legal judgment.

Finding that a table and a paragraph disagree is useful.

Determining whether a financial projection is prudent is not an internal-consistency task.

The product should preserve that boundary in its interface and API.

A completed review means the system finished looking.

It does not mean the document passed.

## Every finding needs a receipt

The best review agent should be easier to audit than the document it reviews.

For every material finding, the system should produce a receipt:

- the exact claim or passage under review
- the type of concern: logical, mathematical, empirical, factual, legal, or editorial
- the source, calculation, tool call, or counterexample behind the concern
- the assumptions required for the critique to hold
- a confidence level and a description of what remains uncertain
- a reproduction path where reproduction is possible
- the model, harness, tools, and version that generated the finding
- the author's or reviewer's disposition: accepted, rejected, revised, or unresolved

That last line matters.

Rejected AI criticism should not vanish. It should remain attached to the record with the reason it was rejected. Otherwise, teams cannot measure recurring false positives, compare model versions, or see when a later reviewer raises the same issue with better evidence.

High-stakes claims also need independence.

If one model helped write the document, another run of the same model family should not be treated as independent verification. Use a different method, a different model or provider, executable checks, and human domain expertise. Preserve disagreements instead of asking a synthesis agent to turn them into one smooth paragraph.

The goal is not consensus-shaped text.

It is inspectable evidence.

## The data policy is part of the review

There is one more difference between the conference pilots and the public product.

The NeurIPS pilot said submissions and feedback would be deleted within seven days after the program. Google says documents uploaded to the public Deep Review service are not used for training, but are stored for one year so users can see them in the interface.

That may be a reasonable product choice.

It is not a footnote for an unpublished theorem, a patent draft, a security analysis, a legal dispute, or a financial plan.

Teams should decide whether the retention period fits the document before uploading it. An API integration should expose retention, deletion, region, access, and audit controls as configuration, not bury them in a policy page.

The system is meant to identify hidden risks inside sensitive documents.

It should not quietly create a new one around them.

## The new bottom line

Deep Review is one of the more useful agent products launched this year because it attacks a real constraint instead of generating more material for the pile.

It has a thoughtful architecture, evidence from real conference pilots, and a sensible first role as a private tool for authors.

The risk is not that the tool is useless.

The risk is that usefulness becomes authority faster than the evidence justifies.

Call it preflight.

Make every finding auditable.

Keep consequential decisions with accountable experts.

Use independent methods for independent verification.

And never turn an API response into a green checkmark just because the word `review` appears in the endpoint.

AI can help scale validation.

Now we need to avoid automating false assurance at the same speed.

## The Short Version

Claude did not break the internet.

That is the first thing to say.

On July 28, Anthropic published research on [discovering cryptographic weaknesses with Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/research/discovering-cryptographic-weaknesses). Using Claude Mythos Preview, Anthropic researchers found an improved attack on HAWK, a post-quantum digital-signature candidate being reviewed in the NIST process, and a faster attack on a reduced-round version of AES.

Anthropic is explicit about the caveat: these findings do not currently affect production systems. HAWK is a candidate scheme, not deployed internet plumbing. The AES result applies to a weakened research version with 7 rounds, not the full 10-round AES-128 people actually depend on.

Good.

But the story is still important.

Not because everyone should panic about passwords.

Not because Claude is now a wizard with a lockpick.

The useful lesson is simpler and more awkward:

AI is making research output cheaper than research validation.

Anthropic says the HAWK attack took about 60 hours of Claude-assisted work, and that each of the two main results cost roughly $100,000 in API spend. The reduced-round AES result took about a week for Mythos to discover, but researchers then spent hundreds of hours learning enough cryptography to validate it. Anthropic says two researchers needed nearly a month to gain confidence in the AES result.

That is the real product shift.

The model can propose, search, code, test, discard, and propose again.

The human still has to know whether the thing is true.

Very futuristic. We automated the hard part and discovered an even harder part behind it.

## This is research acceleration, not magic

Cryptography is a good place to be disciplined about claims.

The field already assumes adversarial review. A new cipher is proposed, attacked, revised, debated, benchmarked, broken, defended, and sometimes standardized only after years of scrutiny. Failure is not embarrassing in the same way it might be in a product launch. Failure is the method.

That is why Anthropic's HAWK result is not simply a scary headline.

HAWK was in a review process designed to surface exactly this kind of weakness before deployment. Anthropic says Claude Mythos found a previously unexploited symmetry in the lattice structure behind HAWK and used it to speed up key recovery. The immediate effect is that some proposed HAWK key sizes appear much weaker than expected, enough that doubling key sizes would be needed to reach the same security level.

That matters for the candidate.

It does not mean post-quantum cryptography is broken.

It does not mean every lattice scheme is doomed.

It means the review process got a new attacker.

That is how cryptography is supposed to work.

The AES result is even easier to misread. Anthropic says Mythos improved attacks on a 7-round version of AES-128, reducing the work of a prior meet-in-the-middle attack by roughly 200 to 800 times depending on how the runtime is measured.

Again: full AES-128 has 10 rounds.

Researchers study reduced-round variants to understand margins, not because your bank is secretly using toy AES.

So the right takeaway is not:

"AI broke encryption."

It is:

"AI is becoming useful at the kind of technical adversarial search that cryptography depends on."

Less dramatic.

More important.

## The agent loop is the instrument

The most interesting part is not that Claude answered a cryptography question.

It is how the work happened.

Anthropic describes a Claude Code-like harness with multiple worker agents, sandboxed tools, Python, Sage, access to published cryptographic work, and enough autonomy to run computational experiments. For HAWK, the human operator was not a lattice-cryptography expert. Anthropic says the human mostly gave project-management guidance: keep track of ideas, use this library, verify the claim.

That is different from asking a chatbot:

"Please find a cryptographic weakness."

The model was not only producing text. It was reading papers, forming hypotheses, writing code, testing ideas, rejecting paths, coordinating sub-agents, and building verification pipelines.

This is the same pattern showing up across AI agents:

The model matters.

The harness matters as much.

The tools matter.

The logs matter.

The ability to run many attempts matters.

The ability to preserve partial ideas matters.

The user is less a prompt writer than a research manager.

That last part is uncomfortable, because it sounds both powerful and extremely easy to overstate. A non-expert with a strong model and a good scaffold can push into expert territory, but the last mile still belongs to expertise. Anthropic's own AES story proves it. The model found the idea quickly. The humans then had to do the slow work of deciding whether the idea was real.

That is not a footnote.

That is the bottleneck.

## Verification becomes the scarce skill

The AI industry keeps talking about "AI scientists."

This is what that phrase looks like when it stops being a slogan.

The output is not a polished answer.

It is a pile of hypotheses, experiments, code, logs, partial proofs, wrong turns, and one suspiciously interesting result.

Someone has to validate it.

Someone has to know the literature.

Someone has to reproduce the attack.

Someone has to check the assumptions.

Someone has to distinguish "new" from "rediscovered."

Someone has to decide whether the result matters under real threat models.

Someone has to coordinate disclosure.

Someone has to tell NIST, authors, vendors, governments, or users without accidentally turning a research finding into an exploit tutorial.

This is the less glamorous future of AI-accelerated science:

the review queue gets longer.

If models can generate plausible research faster than humans can validate it, then the key institutional question is not only how to create more discoveries. It is how to triage them without drowning.

That applies far beyond cryptography.

Drug discovery.

Materials science.

Theorem proving.

Security research.

Biology.

Chip design.

Every field where an AI system can cheaply propose thousands of technical claims will need stronger validation machinery.

The bottleneck moves from imagination to verification.

Beautiful. Peer review was too fast and relaxing anyway.

## Do not confuse confidence with correctness

One of the stranger details in Anthropic's post is that Claude initially resisted the AES work.

Anthropic says early runs had Claude deciding that improving attacks on AES was basically too hard. The researchers pushed it to keep trying, to look for publishable research, and to stop retreating to easier targets. Eventually Mythos produced the Möbius Bridge idea behind the improved reduced-round AES attack.

That is fascinating.

It is also a warning.

The model can be wrong when it is too pessimistic.

The model can be wrong when it is too confident.

The model can be right for reasons that are hard to audit.

The model can create enough technical material that the human feels behind.

This is why "the model thinks it found something" is not a result.

It is a lead.

In software engineering, a lead can often be tested quickly. Does the test pass? Does the exploit reproduce? Does the patch work? Even there, it is messy.

In cryptography, the object is more abstract. A proof can hide a bad assumption. An attack can rely on impossible resources. A result can be mathematically valid and practically irrelevant. A shortcut can be old literature wearing a new name.

So the interface for AI research should not be a victory screen.

It should be an evidence room.

Show the chain of reasoning.

Show the code.

Show failed attempts.

Show reproduction steps.

Show assumptions.

Show where the model is guessing.

Show what a human expert still has to check.

If the AI lab cannot produce that, it has not produced research. It has produced a claim with nice typography.

## The dual-use problem gets sharper

Cryptanalysis is inherently dual-use.

Finding a weakness can make the world safer if it happens before deployment, before attackers exploit it, and before a weak standard gets locked in.

Finding a weakness can also make the world less safe if it applies to a deployed system and spreads faster than defenders can patch or migrate.

Anthropic says it followed responsible disclosure, consulted academics, shared advance copies with U.S. government and industry partners, and coordinated the HAWK disclosure with the scheme authors and the public NIST mailing list.

That is the right shape.

It is also a preview of a much harder problem.

What happens when a model finds a practical weakness in a deployed cryptosystem?

What happens when it finds ten?

What happens when a weaker open model can reproduce the method?

What happens when the relevant maintainers are under-resourced, the migration path is slow, and attackers can run the same search?

This is where the "AI for defense" story stops being comforting.

Yes, capable models can help defenders.

They can also compress the time between discovery and weaponization.

The safe answer is not to pretend the capability does not exist. The safe answer is to build disclosure, triage, access control, logging, and emergency remediation processes before the first truly practical result lands.

Cryptography already has some of this culture.

Most industries do not.

## What builders should take from this

Most product teams are not designing post-quantum signatures.

Still, this story should change how they think about AI in technical work.

If you are using AI for security research, code review, protocol design, compliance analysis, scientific work, or any domain where correctness matters, ask:

- Can the system preserve enough evidence for a human to audit the result?
- Can it reproduce the finding from a clean environment?
- Can it separate speculative leads from verified claims?
- Can it track which source or experiment changed its mind?
- Can multiple agents disagree productively instead of converging on the same mistake?
- Can a human expert inspect the work without reading a billion tokens?
- Can the organization triage model-generated findings by severity, novelty, and confidence?
- Can disclosure happen before public excitement turns into operational risk?

These are product questions.

Not only research questions.

AI tools for expert domains will not be judged only by how many ideas they generate. They will be judged by how well they help humans verify, prioritize, and safely act on those ideas.

The next research assistant should not only say:

"I found something."

It should say:

"Here is the claim, here is the evidence, here is what failed, here is what would falsify it, here is who should review it, and here is why we should not ship a press release yet."

Annoying?

Yes.

Useful?

Extremely.

## The bottom line

Anthropic's cryptography result is a clean signal because it is exciting and limited at the same time.

No production crypto panic.

No instant replacement of experts.

No proof that every model should be handed every cipher.

But also no easy dismissal.

A frontier model, placed in the right agentic scaffold, appears capable of doing serious adversarial research in a field where correctness is hard, stakes are high, and human expertise is scarce.

That should make builders less impressed by raw output and more obsessed with validation.

The next bottleneck is not whether AI can produce more claims.

It can.

The bottleneck is whether humans, institutions, and tools can tell which claims deserve to become reality.
